01 Gender Beyond Identity: Configurations of Transgender Experience Job market paper +

Gender is a complex social structure, yet research often collapses it into gender identity categories, narrowing empirical understandings of gender inequality. Studies incorporating measures of gender presentation, relational context, and structural experience provide new insight but typically include only one dimension in addition to identity. This paper develops an approach for measuring embodied experiences across multiple interlocking dimensions of gender in a sample of 27,000 transgender people. With the 2015 US Transgender Survey and variables that capture gender’s presentational, relational, and institutional construction, I use latent class analysis to identify five classes of transgender experience: Stealth, Visible, Social, Medical, and Inner Transition. These classes identify recognizable subgroups and demonstrate that income and health disparities are patterned by more than identity alone. Conforming to cisgender norms and not being perceived as transgender are associated with higher income, but privacy of transgender status is associated with better health only when gender presentation aligns with identity. This configurational approach offers a blueprint for studying other embodied social constructs.

02 Transition Costs for Whom? A Life Course Perspective on Transgender Income Disparities Manuscript available +

Transgender people in the U.S. experience widespread economic marginalization, but only some transgender people experience an economic penalty following transition. Qualitative research has shown that while women experience an economic cost following transition, men experience a slight insignificant advantage. This paper confirms that finding and uncovers additional heterogeneity in income disparities by transition timing through a framework of disruptive events, a life course perspective, and trans temporalities. Across a dataset of 10,000 transgender men and women with variation in whether and when they changed how they present their gender, women who transitioned at young ages earn less than women who have not transitioned and who transitioned at older ages. Men do not have an income penalty at any age of transition, but there is an insignificant penalty for men who transitioned recently. Further contextualized by employment status and visibility as transgender, these income disparities provide evidence that gender transition’s costs are not the same for all transgender people and accumulate on top of other forms of disadvantage.

03 Transgender Women's Racialized Sex Work: A Demographic Description Manuscript available +

Transgender women of color disproportionately engage in sex work, a stigmatized and criminalized form of work. Due to limited data about both sex work and transgender populations, less is known about who tends to engage in sex work and in what forms. This paper provides a demographic portrait of racialized rates in sex work among the 9,300 transgender women in the 2015 United States Transgender Survey. Across all demographic comparisons, transgender women of color are more likely to have ever engaged in sex work. For transgender women of color, more education is not associated with lower rates of sex work, and over 40% of Black women in this sample have engaged in sex work regardless of education. Returns to education may be racialized: more education is only protective against sex work for white transgender women. Digital sex work is more common for younger women, but at all ages, Black and Hispanic women engage in street-based sex work at higher rates than white women. This suggests sex work is racialized along hierarchies of the sex industry.
